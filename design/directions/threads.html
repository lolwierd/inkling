Inkling, direction 3: Loose Threads

Every question is a loose thread in your note. Inkling stitches it to the words it’s about; you answer it and tie it off.

The object: an embroidery sampler in its hoop

  1. A sampler is a practice cloth: rows of stitches, an alphabet, the maker’s name and the date. Its border was often stitched last, when the piece was done.
  2. Embroidery has a back. The front shows the clean stitch; the back shows every knot and jump, and embroiderers judge work by the back.
  3. A loose end has to be finished: tied off with a knot or woven under. A French knot is how you make a dot. Our words for arguments already come from sewing: losing the thread, tying up loose ends.

Reply in the tag to tie the thread off.

Motion and sound

Motion only ever moves thread, never your words, and typing cancels it. With reduced motion every draw-on becomes a 120 ms fade. Sound is off until you turn it on in the top bar.

How a thread hangs

Drag the tag, or focus it and use the arrow keys. Each thread is a rope of 26 points, simulated with Verlet integration: gravity, a rest length 9% longer than the gap so it sags, and a little stiffness so it never kinks. It comes up through a hole at the edge of the column, never across your words. The same Rope class draws every thread on this page, and it is what the app will run.

What it refuses, and its risk

It refuses

  • Fabric under your words. The weave lives on the cloth around the page. The page you write on is flat, always.
  • Mascots. No needle with eyes, no smiling spool. Inkling is a reader, not a character.
  • Handwriting fonts. Every word is set in a typeface you can read at a glance.
  • Stitching on controls. Buttons stay plain buttons. Thread is for Inkling’s marks only.
  • Motion that blocks typing. Start typing and every animation in the margin stops where it is.
  • Colour as the only signal. A correction carries a cross-stitch, a question does not; every tag says which mind wrote it.

The risk: it can tip into cute

Craft is the world most likely to turn into a scrapbook. What holds it back: texture only off the page; Figtree for every control and every word Inkling says; real stitch geometry (stitches that narrow into the cloth, knots that are actually knots) instead of clip-art; and exact layout, with the same grid as the other two directions. If it still reads as cute in daily use, the first thing to go is the border, not the thread.

13

Things you might have missed

Specific to this direction. The list that applies to all three is on the overview.

  • Colour-blind readers. Five thread colours cannot carry “which mind” alone. Every tag names its mind, and in Ask the room each mind also gets its own stitch: running stitch for Inkling, back stitch (solid) for Skeptic, whipped stitch for Tutor.
  • The border needs an end. After 24 motifs it closes into a full frame, and the note’s title and date are stitched into the bottom corner, the way a sampler is signed. A finished note looks finished.
  • Long notes. Threads far below the fold need the count and ⌥⌘↓ more than anything else; the gutter knots show where they are as you scroll.
  • Turn the work over (later). The back of embroidery shows every jump. A “back of the page” view would list every thread ever pulled on a note, including snipped ones, in the order they were asked.
  • Custom minds pick a thread. The picker offers five colours already checked for 3:1 on both the linen and the lamplight page; a colour that fails in either is not offered.
  • Hover sway nudges the rope and it settles in about 600 ms; under reduced motion it does not move at all. It is the only motion that is not feedback, and the first to cut if it ever distracts.
  • A still page costs nothing. A rope is only simulated while it moves (hover, tie-off, snip, a pin moved by layout). At rest it is a cached path. Long notes simulate only the threads on screen.
  • Threads never cross your words. From the end of the stitching the thread runs under the cloth and comes up through a hole at the edge of the column. If the margin ever sits below the text (a narrow window), the hole moves to the end of the stitching instead.
  • Dock badge. The number of loose threads, off by default.
  • Export and print never include stitches. The optional appendix lists answered threads as “Tied off while writing”.

Order of work

  1. Palette and fonts. Linen and lamplight tokens; Fraunces cut at soft 100 and Figtree bundled. Nothing else looks right until these are in.
  2. Stitches and the gutter. The running stitch, the cross-stitch, the read marks. Pure painting in the editor; no new state.
  3. The rope and the tags. Port Rope, pin it to the hole and the loop, restyle the cards. Check it against this page side by side.
  4. Tie-off, sway and snip. The three animations, then Undo.
  5. The border. Count tied-off threads per note; draw the motifs; close the frame at 24.
  6. Stitched drawings. The same stitch for every stroke, revealed stitch by stitch.
  7. The icon, reworked during the build, then the layered version for macOS 26.

Total: M to L. The look is mostly paths GPUI already draws (PathBuilder fills). The new work is the rope (a small, self-contained port), its three animations, and the border.
