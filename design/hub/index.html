The manifesto

What Inkling believes, the features that follow, and what in today's app it argues against.

This document, written on 6 Nov 2026, has three parts: what Inkling believes, the features those beliefs call for, and what in today's app they argue against. The beliefs are numbered so the later parts can point at them.

1. What Inkling believes

  1. You find out what you think by writing it down, because on the page the numbers have to add up and each step has to follow from the last. That only works if you write the sentence yourself, so Inkling never writes your notes for you. It does not finish your sentences, rewrite your paragraphs, draft a page, summarise into your note, or put its sentence where yours was. A note written for you can be correct and still leave you not knowing what you think.
  2. The most useful thing a reader can do is ask a good question in the right place. When something you wrote does not hold up, Inkling asks about it beside the exact words, and the work stays with you: you decide what you meant, and the sentence that answers it is yours. When a fact is plainly wrong, Inkling says so and shows how it knows, and still leaves the fix to you.
  3. Most of what you write needs no comment, and a reader that remarks on everything teaches you to skip its remarks. Inkling says nothing about most paragraphs, but it marks each one it has read, so its silence tells you the paragraph held up. It waits for you to pause, because a question that lands mid-sentence pulls you out of the sentence. It speaks only in the margin of the page you are on.
  4. The margin lets Inkling respond to what you wrote without touching it. The column holds only your words; Inkling's sit beside them, and you can hide them all and find your page exactly as you left it. When you ask Inkling something directly, it answers plainly, in the margin.
  5. A thread is the right shape for a question about your thinking. It is tied to particular words, so you can check the question against what you wrote. It ends when you answer it, change the words until it no longer applies, or decide the words stand. Until then it stays in view, so the open threads on a page show what you have not worked out yet. A chat about the whole note has no anchor and no end, which is why Inkling keeps chat at the side.
  6. Often the best answer to a question is a change to the sentence it is about. When you edit the words a thread points at, Inkling reads them again, and if the problem has gone, the thread ties itself off. Whatever you work out in the margin belongs in the note, because the note is what is left when the margin is hidden, and putting it there is your part of the work.
  7. Deciding that your words should stand is also an answer. When you have weighed a question and cut its thread, that is a decision, and Inkling should remember it. Inkling is not grading you: it keeps no score, and nothing in the notebook should reward you for going along with it.
  8. Everything Inkling keeps about you or a note is somewhere you can read it: the brief you gave the note, what it has learned about you, and every thread it has opened, including the ones you cut. You will only write half-formed thoughts in a notebook if you know what its reader keeps.

2. What follows from it

These are features the beliefs call for that Inkling does not have yet. They are ranked by how directly the manifesto asks for them and how much they would change writing in Inkling, with size breaking ties. Sizes use the scale in directions/DESIGN.md: S is under a day, M a few days, L a week.

  1. Answer in the note (M)
    • What: A thread also ties itself off when a paragraph you write elsewhere in the note answers it, and its knot points to your answer; an explanation you asked for counts once you have written it in your own words.
    • Why: Belief 6 says what you work out belongs in the note, but today a thread ties itself off only after you edit the quoted words or reply in the margin.
  2. Let it stand (S)
    • What: Dismissing a thread can carry a one-line reason, and Inkling tells every mind which points you let stand, so none of them raises one again.
    • Why: Belief 7 says Inkling should remember a no, but today the minds never see a dismissed thread, so after any edit to that paragraph the next read can ask the same question again.
  3. Threads of your own (S)
    • What: Select some words and hang your own question on them in your colour; no mind answers unless you call one, and you tie it off or cut it like any other thread.
    • Why: Belief 5 says a thread is the right shape for a question about your thinking, and many of those questions are your own, yet today every thread belongs to a mind.
  4. Show its working (M)
    • What: A correction always carries a check you can do yourself, either the arithmetic or a source you can open, and when Inkling has neither it asks a question instead.
    • Why: Belief 2 says Inkling shows how it knows, but today a correction can rest on the model's memory alone, and a confident correction that is wrong can talk you out of a sentence that was right.
  5. Keep the old words (S)
    • What: When you edit the words and a thread ties off, the thread keeps your old words, struck through, above the new ones, in the margin and in exports.
    • Why: Belief 6 says the best answer is often a change to your sentence, but today the thread replaces its quote with your new words, so its record loses the change that answered it.
  6. Write it in (S)
    • What: When your reply in the margin ties a thread off, the knot offers to put your reply, word for word, under its paragraph for you to rework.
    • Why: Belief 6 says what you work out belongs in the note; in the walkthrough film, the reply about the long run ties its thread off, yet the plan never says where the long run starts.
  7. Loose ends (S)
    • What: A single list, in ⌘K or the sidebar, shows every open thread in the notebook, oldest first, each a click away from its words.
    • Why: Belief 5 says the open threads show what you have not worked out yet, but today the sidebar only counts them per note, so an old question is easy to lose.
  8. Turn it over (M)
    • What: The back of a note lists every thread it has had, in order, with how each one ended (tied off, let stand, or still open) and the words before and after.
    • Why: Belief 8 says you can read every thread Inkling has opened, but today a dismissed thread vanishes from every view, and nothing lists a note's threads with how each one ended; directions/DESIGN.md already names this as a later idea.
  9. Read it through (S)
    • What: In a note that waits to be asked, one command reads the whole draft once, paragraph by paragraph, with the same rules and the same silences as reading along.
    • Why: Belief 3 says Inkling waits for your pause, and for some writing the pause comes at the end of the draft; today a waiting note gets threads only for one selection at a time.
  10. Threads between notes (L)
    • What: When something you write contradicts or depends on something in another note, Inkling asks about it on a thread that quotes both and links the two.
    • Why: Belief 2 says Inkling asks when something you wrote does not hold up, and sometimes what does not hold up is the gap between two notes written months apart, which it never reads together.

These are left out because each would contradict a belief:

  • Features that write into your text: finishing sentences, rewriting or "improving" a

paragraph, drafting, summarising into the note, fixing spelling or grammar in place, and suggesting titles. Belief 1 rules out all of them.

  • An Apply button on corrections. Typing the fix yourself is the point, and it is what lets the

thread tie itself off (beliefs 1 and 6).

  • Drawings become figures, planned as item 7 of the UX core in directions/DESIGN.md. It would

put Inkling's drawing into the column as part of your note (belief 4). A figure you draw yourself would not conflict.

  • Reminders, notifications, and the optional Dock badge for open questions that

directions/DESIGN.md suggests. Inkling speaks only in the margin of the page you are on (belief 3).

  • Streaks, scores, and any count of what Inkling caught (belief 7).

3. What it argues against in today's app

These run from the most direct conflict to the mildest.

  1. Make it a note copies Inkling's words into the body of a new note. The new note's paragraphs are the thread's messages, each starting "Inkling:" or "Me:", set in your typeface; a headless run against the fake brain confirms it. Beliefs 1 and 4 keep the column for your words alone, and after one edit nothing marks those sentences as Inkling's. The new note could open with your quoted words and the thread hanging in its margin, above an empty page.
  2. Copy and export carry the threads unless you stop them. Copy as Markdown (⇧⌘C) always includes every thread, even while threads are hidden, and the export dialog opens with threads switched on. Belief 4 promises your page exactly as you left it when the margin is hidden, and a copy or an export is that page leaving the notebook. directions/DESIGN.md had marked this export as optional and said export should never print Inkling's marks unless asked. Copy could follow Hide threads, and export could start with threads off.
  3. About you learns by default and does not say what it learned. Learning is on in a new notebook: Inkling rewrites your profile from replies and chat messages in which you mention yourself, and that profile goes into every question every mind asks. The profile sits in Settings, as belief 8 asks, but the toast says only that something was noted, so a new line stays out of sight unless you go looking and remember what the profile said before. This is the one place where Inkling writes about you rather than to you, which makes it the place where belief 8 matters most. The toast could show the new line with Undo, or learning could ask first.
  4. The border counts only tied-off threads. The border along the top of the page grows one motif for each thread tied off. A dismissed thread adds nothing, and a note that held up on the first read has no motifs at all. So the border grows when you go along with a question, by answering it or changing your words, and not when you decide your words should stand. A note that needed many questions also looks more finished than one that needed none. Belief 7 rules out rewards like these. The manifesto keeps the border, which is the signature of the Loose Threads design, and asks only that it count differently: it could mark a note as worked through once nothing in it is open, however many questions that took, and treat a point you let stand the same as one you tied off.
  5. A dismissed thread disappears. It stays in the note's file, but once the eight seconds of Undo have passed no view shows it, and no mind is ever told about it. Beliefs 7 and 8 ask for your no to be remembered and for every thread to stay readable; Let it stand and Turn it over, above, would fix this.
  6. Chat has no anchor and no end. It is the one surface without the thread's shape: its answers point at your sentences but cannot hang on them, and nothing in it is ever finished. Belief 5 keeps chat at the side rather than removing it. A point a chat answer makes about particular words should be able to become a thread on those words.
